# Talking with your kids

The software gives you visibility. What you do with it decides whether this helps.

## Be upfront about monitoring

Tell them before they start: you can read their chats, here's why, and here's what you're looking for (safety, honesty, learning), and what you're not (policing every silly question). Secret monitoring that gets discovered costs more trust than it ever bought. The system prompts in `prompts/` tell the AI to say the same thing.

## Explain what AI actually is

Kids reason better about a tool they understand. Worth covering, at their level:
- It predicts plausible text. It can be confidently wrong. Check anything important.
- It isn't a person or a friend, even when it sounds like one.
- It doesn't keep secrets, and anything typed into it (here or anywhere else) can be seen by someone.

## Show them how instructions work

Every AI they meet runs on hidden instructions someone else wrote. Seeing that once makes the rest of AI make more sense, from "why is this chatbot so nice to me" to "why won't it answer that".

The mode presets in [openwebui/presets.md](../openwebui/presets.md) (Pirate, Coach, Professor) make it visible:
1. In a new chat, pick their everyday helper, then add a mode next to it with the **+** beside the model name. Both answer every message, side by side.
2. Ask the same question to both ("why is the sky blue?"). Let them guess what the second one was told to do.
3. Then read the instructions together (they're in the preset's description).
4. Point out what didn't change: the facts, and the rules you set (the age prompt sits above every mode).

Later: have them write a mode of their own on paper, and you add it. That's all a "skill" is, too.

## Set house rules together

Some starting points:
- Homework: AI can explain, quiz, and give feedback. The work you turn in is yours.
- Personal info never goes in: full name, address, school, passwords, photos.
- If something it says is weird, scary, or confusing, come tell us. You won't be in trouble for what the AI said.
- Using AI somewhere else (a friend's phone, an app) is something we talk about, not something to hide.

## Read chats with curiosity

When you review, look for what they're curious about, where they struggle, and how they treat the tool. Bring up interesting things positively ("I saw you asked about black holes, want to go to the planetarium?") at least as often as concerns. If every mention of the logs is a reprimand, you're teaching them to hide.

## When something concerning shows up

Stay calm, lead with questions, and separate "the AI said something bad" from "you did something wrong." For anything involving self-harm, abuse, or an adult contacting them, involve the appropriate help: pediatrician, school counselor, or in the US, the 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988).

## Graduate trust over time

Monitoring should shrink as judgment grows. One possible path:
- **Younger kids:** smaller model, tight prompt, tools off, weekly review.
- **Middle school:** stronger model, some tools on, spot checks and alerts.
- **High school:** you review on alerts or by agreement, and talk about eventually moving to their own accounts on mainstream products when they're old enough.

Write the path down with them. Kids handle rules better when they can see how to earn their way out of them.
